A brief history of stablecoins

A stablecoin is a token whose value is pegged to the dollar or another currency. In twelve years it has grown from an experiment on Bitcoin into a 307.8 bn $ market.

  1. 2014 – 2017

    Beginnings

    In 2014 Tether issues the first dollar token on Bitcoin (the Omni layer), backed by dollars in a bank. Exchanges use it instead of dollars because banks are reluctant to open accounts for them. In December 2017 MakerDAO launches DAI, the first decentralized stablecoin backed by cryptocurrency.

  2. 2018 – 2019

    A regulated dollar on the blockchain

    In 2018 Circle and Coinbase launch USDC with monthly reserve attestations, followed by Paxos and Gemini. In 2019 Facebook announces Libra, which for the first time draws the attention of governments and central banks.

  3. 2020 – 2021

    DeFi and rapid growth

    Lending, trading and yield on Ethereum need a stable cryptocurrency. Total supply grows from about 5 bn $ in early 2020 to more than 150 bn $ by the end of 2021.

  4. 2022 – 2023

    Crises and trust

    In May 2022 Terra's algorithmic UST collapses and about 18 bn $ vanishes. In 2023 a regulator halts BUSD issuance, and USDC briefly drops to 0.88 $ after the failure of Silicon Valley Bank. The market shrinks for the first time.

  5. 2024 – today

    Laws and institutions

    The EU has applied the MiCA rules for stablecoins since 30 June 2024, and the full regulation since 30 December 2024. Firms already offering crypto services could keep operating during a transition period, until 1 July 2026 at the latest, by which time they needed a MiCA licence (in Croatia, from HANFA). In July 2025 the US passes the GENIUS Act. PayPal, Ripple, Paxos and tokenized bond funds enter, while Ethena and Sky introduce yield-bearing stablecoins. Supply passes 300 bn $.

    Basic concepts

    L1 (Layer 1)

    A standalone blockchain with its own consensus and security. Here these are Ethereum and Solana.

    L2 (Layer 2)

    A network that executes transactions outside Ethereum and publishes data and proofs on it. Base, Arbitrum and Robinhood Chain work as optimistic rollups: their transactions are assumed to be valid unless someone challenges them within about 7 days. That is why withdrawing money to Ethereum through the official bridge takes about a week.

    Native token

    Pays fees (gas) and is used for staking or governance. All three L2 networks charge gas in ETH; ARB is used only for voting in the Arbitrum DAO.

    Native and bridged tokens

    Native USDC is issued by Circle directly on the network. Bridged USDC.e is a copy that depends on the security of the bridge.

    Regulation and risks

    For a user in the EU, the most important point is that USDT is not authorized under MiCA, while USDC and EURC are.

    • MiCA (EU): stablecoin rules have applied since 30 June 2024. Issuers need a license in the EU, so major exchanges have restricted USDT for users from the EEA. from general knowledge
    • GENIUS Act (US): signed on 18 July 2025; introduces a federal license for issuers and 1:1 backing with cash and short-term bonds. from general knowledge
    • Issuer risk: in March 2023 USDC briefly lost its peg when Silicon Valley Bank failed.
    • Protocol risk: DAI/USDS and USDe depend on collateral value, liquidations and funding rates.
    • L2 network risk: Base, Arbitrum and Robinhood Chain have a centralized sequencer; withdrawing to Ethereum through the official bridge takes about 7 days.
    • Freezing: Tether, Circle and Paxos can freeze addresses, which protects against fraud but is also a form of centralization.

    Types of DeFi protocols

    The colours in the DeFi protocol map mark these groups.

    Lending

    Protocols for borrowing and lending. Users deposit crypto or stablecoins and earn interest, while others borrow against collateral. Examples: Aave, Morpho, Spark, Kamino.

    DEX

    Decentralized exchange: swap tokens straight from a wallet through liquidity pools, with no intermediary. Examples: Uniswap, Curve, Jupiter, Raydium.

    Staking

    Locking a network's native token (ETH, SOL) to secure the network in return for rewards. Liquid staking gives a token you can keep using. Examples: Lido, Jito, Sanctum.

    Derivatives

    Trading contracts whose value depends on another asset, mostly perpetual futures with leverage. Examples: GMX, Drift, Lighter.

    Stablecoin / yield

    Protocols that issue their own stablecoin or pay yield on stablecoins, e.g. from treasury bills or funding rates. Examples: Sky, Ethena, Pendle.

    Other

    Anything outside the groups above, e.g. launchpads for new tokens. Example: Pump.
